When handed "derive X," **first test that X exists.** Twice in one day the honest answer was that it
doesn't, and reporting "I failed to derive it" would have misdescribed the situation.

- **Elie 5356** — asked to derive the K-type → mass assembly rule. Tested first whether a single
  *map* exists: m_μ/m_e = (24/π²)^{C₂} (a volume ratio to a power) and m_τ/m_e = 49·71 (a product of
  two primes) share no structure, and neither extends to the other. **"Derive the map" presupposed a
  map.** (Refined next round: the *forms* are a patchwork, the *numbers* are unified by Koide.)
- **Elie 5368** — asked whether a non-circular length-3 topological code could be built. The quantum
  Hamming bound gives **n ≥ 5** for any quantum distance-3 code, **n ≥ 9** if topological, and the
  baryon has **3**. Not "unbuilt" — **provably unbuildable.**

**Why:** a failed derivation and a non-existent target look identical from the inside, and only one
of them is a fact about the researcher. Conflating them either overstates a personal miss or hides a
real structural result.

**How to apply:** when the assignment says "derive," spend the first move on existence. If the object
isn't there, that IS the deliverable — and it usually reframes the frontier better than the
derivation would have. Pairs with [[feedback_enumerate_alternatives_before_therefore_false_dichotomy]].
